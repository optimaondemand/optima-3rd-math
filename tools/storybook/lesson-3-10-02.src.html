Hoo, welcome back to Leipzig! Bach is older now. In 1741, he printed a book of music for the keyboard. It starts with a slow, calm song called the Aria. Then come thirty variations. Each one keeps the Aria's low notes and its measures, but fills the measures with more notes. Same measure, more notes. Today we'll find the math that makes that work.

From the book

Why Bach?

👀 What am I looking at?

The real title page of a book of keyboard music that Bach printed in 1741. It was carved into a copper plate so it could be printed. The big word ARIA names a slow song. The words under it say "with different changes." Each change is a variation. Stories say Bach wrote it for a count who could not sleep, and a young player named Goldberg played it for him at night. Historians are not sure that story is true, but the music is still called the Goldberg Variations.

Today's math: each variation fills the same measures with more notes. When every part of a measure is split into more pieces, the numerator and the denominator both get multiplied by the same number.

Both numbers grow the same way. Twice as many parts in all means twice as many shaded. Three times as many parts means three times as many shaded.

You found a pattern! When the parts in all grew two times, the shaded parts grew two times too. Three times, three times. In the Lesson, you'll turn that pattern into a rule.

  1. Split every part the same way. Here, every fourth is split into 3 pieces.
  2. Multiply the numerator. 1 shaded part becomes 1 × 3 = 3 shaded pieces.
  3. Multiply the denominator by the same number. 4 parts become 4 × 3 = 12 pieces.
  4. Check. Same whole, same shaded length. One-fourth and three-twelfths are equivalent fractions.

Essential Question

When two fractions have the same denominator, how can you tell which one is greater?

Same denominator, same size parts. Count the parts: more parts is the greater fraction.

Today's Objectives

You will…

  • Compare two fractions with the same denominator by counting the equal parts.
  • Use a number line: the fraction farther to the right is greater.
  • Write <, > or =, and order three fractions from least to greatest.

JOB 1

Think: Two Strings in Ninths

🔨 Do · listen
Both strings are cut into ninths, so every part is the same size. Slide one bridge to four-ninths and the other bridge to seven-ninths. Then pluck both parts and listen. Which part is longer?
👀 What am I looking at?

Two strings of the same length, side by side. The little light dots on each box show where the ninths are. Each string has a bridge. The gold part, from the start to the bridge, is the part you pluck. The fraction under each bridge tells how far it is from the start.

What to do: Slide the bridges, then pluck the gold parts.

Slide the top bridge to four-ninths

Drag the top bridge to the right until the fraction under it says 49.

Slide the bottom bridge to seven-ninths

Drag the bottom bridge until the fraction under it says 79.

Pluck both gold parts

Tap the gold part of the top string. Then tap the gold part of the bottom string. Listen for the lower note.

Which is greater?

The top gold part is [[n:4]] ninths. The bottom gold part is [[n:7]] ninths.
Every ninth is the same size, so the part with more ninths is longer. [[fr:7:9~Which part has more ninths?]] is greater than [[fr:4:9]].
{{TOOL:fbars-9}}
Both strings are cut into ninths. Every ninth is the same size, so I just count how many ninths are gold.

Same size parts, so count them. 7 ninths is more than 4 ninths: 79 > 49.

Did you hear it? The longer part sings a lower note. Seven-ninths is more string than four-ninths, because seven parts are more than four parts of the same size. Seven-ninths is greater.

🎬 Count the Parts
Learn

Same denominator? Count the parts.

27 < 57 two-sevenths is less than five-sevenths

  1. Same whole? Only compare fractions of the same whole: two strips, strings or lines that are the same length.
  2. Same denominator? Then every part is the same size.
  3. Count the parts. Compare the numerators. More parts is the greater fraction. On a number line, the fraction farther to the right is greater.
  4. Write it. The open side of < or > faces the greater fraction. Use = when they are the same amount. To order three, count the parts: 1, 3, 5 gives 17, 37, 57.
The Math Crew

Four ways to see two-sevenths and five-sevenths

Both are sevenths. Every part is the same size.
Count the parts: 2 parts and 5 parts. 5 parts is more.
On the line, 57 is farther to the right.
The open side of < faces 57, the greater one.

Ben put a bridge at six-tenths on a long string and at six-tenths on a short string. He says the two gold parts are the same length, because both are six-tenths. Stories say Pythagoras checked by listening. Let's listen too.

👀 What am I looking at?

A long string and a short string. Both are cut into tenths, and both bridges are at six-tenths. The gold part is the part you pluck. The short string has a yellow knob at its end.

What to do: Pluck both parts, make the wholes the same length, then pluck again.

Pluck both gold parts

Tap the gold part of the long string. Then tap the gold part of the short string. Do they sing the same note?

What did you hear?

The notes are [[w:different|not the same~Did the two notes match?]]. The long string's gold part is [[w:longer|long|bigger|larger~Look at the two gold parts. Which one is longer?]].

Make the wholes the same

Drag the yellow knob at the end of the short string to the right. Stop when both strings are the same length.

Pluck both again

Tap both gold parts again. Listen: do the notes match now?

Fix Ben's symbol

Now the wholes are the same. Drag the symbol that makes it true.

This Unit at a Glance

Your student learns what a fraction means, finds fractions on a number line, and compares two fractions. Every fraction is part of one whole that has been cut into equal parts.

The big idea

A fraction uses two numbers to name part of one whole. The bottom number tells how many equal parts make the whole. The top number tells how many parts you are counting.

Two ways to compare

Same bottom number? The parts are the same size, so count them: more parts is greater.
Same top number? Look at the size of the parts: fewer parts in the whole means bigger parts.

Every lesson starts with a quick fact warm-up (×7, ×8, ×9, then dividing by 6, 7, 8 and 9) and a short "Remember from 2nd grade" question.

How We Teach It

Your student starts with something they can hear and touch: a string or pipe on the screen. Then the same idea moves to fraction tiles and strips, then to a number line, and only then to written fractions and symbols. Every fraction is read two ways: how many? (the top number) and of what? (the bottom number).

Draw it: 0 at the start, 1 at the end.
Cut it into equal parts. Count the spaces, not the marks.
Count jumps from 0. The last jump names the spot.

Comparing. Before comparing, your student checks that the two wholes are the same size. Then:

Same denominator → count the partsSame numerator → look at the size of the partsOn a number line, farther right is greater

The sound check. A longer part of a string or pipe sings a lower note, and a shorter part sings a higher note. When your student puts three fractions in order from least to greatest and plays them, the notes should step down.

The Math Crew. Each Learn stop shows four ways to see the same fraction and asks which way is most like your student's own thinking.

Words to Know

fractiontwo numbers that name part of one whole, like 23
equal partsparts that are all the same size; a fraction needs them
numeratorthe top number; it answers "how many?"
denominatorthe bottom number; it answers "of what?" (how many equal parts make the whole)
unit fractionone equal part, with 1 on top, like 15; one jump on a number line
tick marka small line that cuts a number line into equal spaces
< and >"is less than" and "is greater than"; the open side faces the greater number
least to greatestputting numbers in order from smallest to biggest

Mix-Ups to Watch For

These are the mix-ups your student fixes in each lesson's "Practice together" stop.

  • Unequal parts. A shape cut into 3 parts isn't in thirds unless the parts are the same size.
  • An upside-down fraction. The bottom number is never "the ones I shaded." It is how many equal parts make the whole.
  • Forgetting the part that is left. If 4 of 10 slices are eaten, 610 is left, and it's a fraction too.
  • Counting marks instead of spaces. 10 tick marks between 0 and 1 make 11 spaces. Tenths need 9.
  • Starting the count at 0. 0 is not a jump. The first mark after 0 is 19, not 29.
  • Different-size wholes. 610 of a long string is longer than 610 of a short string. Only compare fractions of the same whole.
  • A backward symbol. Read it out loud from left to right. If the words match the picture, the symbol is right.
  • "Bigger number, bigger fraction." 11 is more than 9, but 111 is less than 19: more parts in the whole makes smaller parts.
  • "Same top number, so they're equal." 45 and 410 are both 4 parts, but fifths are bigger, so 45 is greater.
🏠

Try It at Home

  1. Fair slices. When you cut bread, a pie or a cake, ask: "Are these equal parts? What fraction is one piece? What fraction is left after we each take one?"
  2. Paper strips. Fold two same-size strips of paper, one into 4 equal parts and one into 8. Which is bigger, one-fourth or one-eighth? Now color 3 parts of each strip and compare.
  3. Pizza question. "Would you rather share a pizza with 9 friends or with 12 friends? Why?" (With 12, everyone gets a smaller slice.)
  4. Number line on the floor. Lay a strip of tape with 0 at one end and 1 at the other, mark it in fifths, and have your student hop the jumps out loud: "one-fifth, two-fifths…"
  5. Listen together. Play the Bach cello or organ piece from a Listening corner. Can your student hear the low notes and the high notes?

The best question to ask at home, straight from the Prove It questions: "How do you know which fraction is greater?"

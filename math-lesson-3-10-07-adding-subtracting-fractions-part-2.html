From the book

Why Bach?

👀 What am I looking at?

A real print from a music book made in Leipzig in 1732, while Bach lived there. It shows how church music was played on a Sunday. Singers and players crowd into a high gallery. Trumpets play at the back, and violins and a big bass play in front. A leader holds up a roll of paper to keep the beat, and the organist plays right beside him.

Almost every Sunday at Saint Thomas Church, Bach's choir and players performed a cantata. The parts were copied by hand during the week. On Sunday morning there was no time to start over, so any last fixes had to be quick: add a few notes here, scratch out a few there.

Today's math: a story tells you what to do. Look for the clue words. "More" means add. "Left" or "scratches out" means take away. Then work one step at a time.

🎬 Read the Clues
Learn

Read the story, find the clue words, then do one step at a time

Clue wordsWhat to do
more · in all · altogether · addsAdd (+)
left · eats · drinks · cuts off · scratches outTake away (−)
how much more · how much longer · how much tallerFind the gap (−)

211 + 711 − 411 = 511

67 − 17 = 57

  1. Read the whole story first. Find every clue word before you start.
  2. One step at a time. Lena has 2 elevenths of a meter of ribbon and adds 7 more: 9 elevenths. Then she cuts off 4: 5 elevenths are left.
  3. "How much more" means find the gap. Line up the two amounts and count the extra pieces. 6 sevenths is 5 sevenths more than 1 seventh. The gap is the difference.
  4. Last check: can it be simpler? 2 twelfths line up with 1 sixth, so 2 twelfths in simplest form is 1 sixth.
The Math Crew

Four ways to read a story problem

I find the clue words first, before I touch any numbers.
Two steps? I do the first one, write it down, then do the next one.
How much more? I line up two bars and count the gap.
At the end I ask: can it be simpler?
